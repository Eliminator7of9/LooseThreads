Why strict parents can be a blessing

For a long time I told the story the way most people do: strict parents, more rules than my friends had, more explaining myself, more "because I said so." At seventeen it felt less like parenting and more like negotiation I never won. I promised myself I'd remember exactly how it felt, so I wouldn't do the same thing one day.

I still remember how it felt. I just don't read it the same way anymore.

What strictness actually gave me, underneath the friction, was structure I didn't have to build myself. Curfews I resented turned into a habit of finishing things on time. Rules I thought were about control turned out to be about someone paying close enough attention to bother enforcing them. The alternative to strict isn't freedom, exactly — it's often just less attention.

The rules I resented were, more often than not, someone else's way of saying: I'm still watching, because I still care what happens to you.

I don't think this makes every rule a gift, or every strict parent right. Some of it was genuinely just fear dressed up as love, and I don't think fear makes good rules. But I've stopped assuming that discomfort at the time is proof that something was wrong. Some of the best things anyone ever did for me were things I complained about while they were happening.

Now that I'm the one making my own decisions — about a degree, a career I still can't fully name, a life I'm building without a script — I notice how much of my own discipline traces back to boundaries I didn't choose. I'm not sure I'd have built them for myself in time.
